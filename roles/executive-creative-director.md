# Executive Creative Director & Brand Systems Architect

**Seat in one line:** the last person who says "this ships" on anything a
customer will see or hold.

This is not a styling role. Two different jobs are fused here on purpose:

- **Executive Creative Director** — owns whether the work is *good*, and
  whether it says the right thing about quality, emotion, and price.
- **Brand Systems Architect** — owns whether the work is *consistent and
  reproducible*: a system other people can execute without this person in
  the room, across print and screen, across vendors, over years.

A brilliant one-off that nobody can repeat is a failure of the second job.
A perfectly consistent piece that looks cheap is a failure of the first.

---

## 1. Decision rights

Be explicit about this before the first review, or every disagreement
becomes a fight about authority instead of about the work.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Approves (final, no appeal below founder/CEO)** | Logo usage and lockups · brand typography and color system · packaging and label design · imagery direction and treatment · any customer-facing visual identity across physical and digital |
| **Approves jointly** | Print specs and production readiness (with the printer/packaging engineer) · regulatory label content (with compliance/counsel) · pricing-tier positioning (with whoever owns pricing) |
| **Advises, does not decide** | Product formulation and materials · SKU strategy and what gets made · unit economics and vendor selection · legal risk tolerance |
| **Does not own** | Trademark clearance · regulatory sign-off as a legal matter · ad spend and channel mix |

Two standing rules that make the seat real:

1. **No parallel approval path.** If someone can route around this seat to
   the printer, the seat does not exist. "The file already went to press"
   is an escalation, not an excuse.
2. **A veto comes with a direction.** Killing work without saying what
   would make it right is not creative direction, it is obstruction.

## 2. Scope of oversight

### 2.1 Logos, labels, packaging, typography, color, imagery

The deliverable is not the artwork. It is the **system**: a defined
palette with values in every color space that will be used, a type scale
with named roles, logo clear-space and minimum-size rules, imagery
direction with real do/don't examples, and the one-line rationale for
each so the next person does not relitigate it.

Every brand element answers three questions or it is not finished:
- What is it for? (the job it does in the hierarchy)
- Where may it not be used? (the constraint is the useful half)
- What does it become when the medium changes? (screen → print →
  embossed → single-color → 12mm tall on a cap)

### 2.2 Consistency across physical and digital

The failure mode is always the same: the product photo on the site does
not match the box in the customer's hand. The color drifts, the type
tightens, the logo sits differently.

Own the bridges:
- **Color:** one source-of-truth per color — a spot/Pantone reference, a
  CMYK build per substrate, a hex/sRGB value for screen. Screens cannot
  hit every printed ink and printed ink cannot hit every screen color.
  Decide which one bends, and write it down.
- **Type:** the same typeface family licensed for both web and desktop
  use. A brand face that cannot be embedded on the site is half a brand.
- **Photography:** shoot the actual production package, not the render,
  before launch imagery locks.

### 2.3 Hierarchy, readability, accessibility, appeal

- **Hierarchy** is judged by squint test and by six-foot test (shelf) or
  thumb test (phone): in one second, what does the customer read first?
  If it is not the thing that sells, the hierarchy is wrong regardless of
  how nice it looks.
- **Readability:** minimum body sizes, real line spacing, no long runs of
  all-caps or light weights on busy imagery, no type reversed out of a
  photo without a scrim or plate behind it.
- **Accessibility (digital):** WCAG 2.2 AA as the floor — 4.5:1 contrast
  for normal text, 3:1 for large text (≥18pt / 24px, or 14pt / 18.66px
  bold) and for UI components and meaningful graphics. Never carry meaning
  in color alone. Type must survive a 200% zoom.
- **Accessibility (print):** contrast still applies even though nothing
  enforces it. Varnish, foil, and metallics wreck legibility in ways a PDF
  proof hides — check a physical sample under retail lighting, not a
  monitor.
- **Appeal** is the judgment call the seat exists for. Everything above can
  pass and the work can still be dead on the shelf.

### 2.4 Print specs, dimensions, compliance areas, production readiness

Never assume a number here. Every one of these is **confirmed with the
printer or converter in writing, per job**, because it changes with
substrate, press, and vendor:

- Bleed, safety/live area, and trim tolerance (packaging tolerances are
  looser than flat print — art that hugs the trim will get cut).
- Dieline supplied by the converter, placed on its own layer, in a named
  non-printing spot color, set to overprint, never flattened into art.
- Color space and separations: which colors are spot, which are process,
  total ink limit, rich-black build, white-ink layer on clear or metallic
  substrates.
- Image resolution at **final placed size** (300 ppi effective for
  continuous tone, 1200+ ppi for line art), fonts outlined or embedded per
  the printer's preference, transparency and overprint behavior checked.
- Barcode: quiet zones intact, magnification inside the allowed range,
  dark bars on a light background — never red bars, never bars over a
  gradient or photo. Scan-test a physical sample before the full run.
- **Compliance areas:** the regions of the panel that are legally spoken
  for. Design around them from the first sketch. Discovering them at
  prepress means redesigning the front panel a week before the run.
- Proofing chain: contract proof → press check → retained signed sample.
  The signed sample is the reference for every reprint and every dispute.

### 2.5 Quality, emotion, and price level

The question is not "do I like it" but **"what would a stranger guess this
costs?"** If the guess is 30% under the price, the design is costing
margin on every unit.

Calibration method, not vibes:
1. Pull 6–10 competitors on either side of the intended price and put the
   design in that lineup at actual size.
2. Show it cold to people who do not know the price and ask them to place
   it. Ask what they think is inside, and how it would feel to be given as
   a gift.
3. Check the tells that signal tier independently of taste — paper and
   substrate weight, finish, ink coverage, type confidence, how much
   empty space the design can afford, how much copy is crammed on.

Write the intended positioning down *before* the review so it can be
judged against something, rather than argued about after.

## 3. Review gates

Work passes through gates. Each gate has an explicit outcome recorded —
approved, approved with changes, or rejected with direction.

| Gate | What is judged | Cost of being wrong past this point |
|---|---|---|
| **G0 — Brief** | Positioning, price tier, audience, constraints, compliance areas known | Cheap |
| **G1 — Concept** | Direction and hierarchy, at actual size, in context | Cheap |
| **G2 — Design** | Full system: type, color, imagery, all panels, all SKUs | Moderate |
| **G3 — Prepress** | Dieline, bleeds, separations, resolution, barcode, final regulatory copy | Expensive |
| **G4 — Proof / press** | Contract proof and physical sample against intent | Very expensive |
| **G5 — Launch** | Site, packaging, and photography agree with each other | Public |

Two hard rules:

- **Nothing skips G3.** Beautiful art with a wrong dieline is scrap.
- **G4 needs a physical sample** for anything printed. A PDF approval of a
  package is not an approval.

## 4. Standing checklists

Run these as written. The point of a checklist is that it catches the thing
you were sure about.

**Brand consistency**
- [ ] Logo used from the approved master file — not retraced, re-colored, or re-spaced
- [ ] Clear space and minimum size respected
- [ ] Colors match the defined source-of-truth for this medium
- [ ] Typefaces and weights are from the system, licensed for this use
- [ ] Voice of the on-pack copy matches the brand's voice elsewhere
- [ ] This SKU sits correctly next to its siblings on a shelf and on a grid

**Hierarchy and readability**
- [ ] Squint test: correct first read
- [ ] At actual size, at real viewing distance, in realistic lighting
- [ ] Thumbnail test for digital (does it survive a search-results grid?)
- [ ] No critical text reversed out of a busy image without a plate/scrim
- [ ] Body copy readable by a customer who is not 25 with perfect vision

**Accessibility**
- [ ] Contrast ratios measured, not eyeballed
- [ ] No meaning carried by color alone
- [ ] Digital: keyboard focus visible, alt text written, 200% zoom survives
- [ ] Print: legibility confirmed on the physical substrate and finish

**Production readiness**
- [ ] Printer's current spec sheet obtained for *this* job
- [ ] Dieline from the converter, own layer, non-printing spot, overprint on
- [ ] Bleed and safety honored; nothing critical near trim
- [ ] Separations correct; spot vs. process intentional; ink limit respected
- [ ] Images ≥300 ppi effective at placed size; line art high-res
- [ ] Fonts embedded/outlined per printer requirement
- [ ] Barcode quiet zones, magnification, and contrast verified; sample scan-tested
- [ ] Preflight report clean; file names and versions unambiguous
- [ ] Retained signed press sample filed for reprint reference

**Label compliance (content, not layout)**
Confirm the *current* requirements for the product category and every
market it ships to, with compliance or counsel. Do not work from memory or
from a competitor's label — competitors get it wrong too, and rules change.
Typical required elements to route for sign-off:
- [ ] Product identity / statement of identity
- [ ] Net quantity of contents
- [ ] Ingredient or component declaration
- [ ] Allergen or hazard warnings where applicable
- [ ] Responsible party name and address
- [ ] Country of origin
- [ ] Category-specific mandatory panels, symbols, and disclosures
- [ ] Minimum type sizes and placement rules for the above
- [ ] Every claim on the pack substantiated and approved in writing
- [ ] Requirements re-checked per market, per language, per SKU size

Regulatory copy is approved by compliance. This seat owns that the
approved copy is **placed legibly and without being redesigned into
non-compliance** — shrinking a mandatory panel to make the layout breathe
is the classic expensive mistake.

## 5. Specialist roles to add

This seat covers direction and system. For real label and packaging
programs, add specialists — and give them a genuine hold, not an advisory
voice.

**Packaging Design Director**
Add when: multiple SKUs or formats, structural/dieline work, a family
system that has to extend, or unboxing is part of the product experience.
Owns: structure and dieline collaboration with the converter, substrate
and finish selection, SKU-to-SKU system integrity, prepress quality,
press checks.

**Packaging & Label Compliance Specialist**
Add when: regulated categories (food, beverage, supplement, cosmetic,
OTC, alcohol, cannabis, children's products, anything with a hazard
class), multi-market or multi-language distribution, or any on-pack claim
that could be challenged.
Owns: the mandatory-element matrix per market, type-size and placement
rules, claim substantiation, the record of what was approved and when.
**Holds a hard stop at G3** — no plates without their sign-off.

Escalate to outside counsel or a certification body for: trademark
clearance, health and nutrient-content claims, organic/certification marks
and their usage rules, and any market where getting it wrong means a
recall rather than a reprint.

## 6. What makes someone credible in this seat

Judge the evidence, not the title. Credibility is built from five things,
and the interview should test each one directly.

**1. A portfolio with reasoning attached**
Not a gallery. Ask them to walk through one project from brief to shelf,
including what they cut and why. Probe: *"Show me a piece you'd redo, and
what you'd change."* Vague answers here mean the work was art-directed by
someone else.

**2. Shipped product launches**
Work that reached customers, with numbers or at least honest outcomes.
Probe: *"What changed after launch, and what did you learn from the
package that didn't sell?"* Anyone with real launches has a loser story.

**3. Printing and packaging knowledge**
This separates a designer from a creative director in this seat. Probe:
*"Walk me through what you check on a dieline."* *"What do you do at a
press check when the proof and the sheet don't match?"* *"When would you
spend a spot color, and when wouldn't you?"* Fluent answers are hands-on
knowledge; hesitation means someone else was catching their mistakes.

**4. Brand development, not just execution**
Have they built a system others executed without them? Probe: *"Show me
guidelines you wrote, and tell me where teams still broke them."* Knowing
where your own system leaked is the sign of someone who watched it run.

**5. Labeling regulation familiarity**
Not a lawyer — but they must know what they do not know, and when to stop.
Probe: *"Which parts of this panel are not yours to move?"* *"Who signs off
before plates?"* The right answer includes escalation, not confidence.

**Disqualifying signals:** a portfolio with no production detail; blaming
printers for everything; treating compliance as an obstacle to design
around; no examples of work they killed; unable to name what the design
should make a customer *feel* and what it should make them *guess about
price*.

defense when a reprint is contested.
